Material Thickness and Joint Design

Sheet materials are usually sold using a nominal thickness. A sheet labeled as 3 mm plywood, for example, may not actually measure exactly 3.00 mm thick. Manufacturing variation, coatings, veneers, and moisture can all affect the real thickness.

This becomes particularly important when designing parts that fit together.

Tabs and Slots

A common laser-cut construction method uses tabs on one part that fit into slots on another.

MEDIA PLACEHOLDER: Diagram or photo of a laser-cut tab-and-slot joint.

If the slot is too small, the pieces will not fit. If it is too large, the joint will be loose. The correct dimensions depend on both the actual material thickness and the kerf produced by the laser.

For Projects Where Fit Matters

  1. Measure the actual material.
  2. Design the joint based on that measurement.
  3. Cut a small test joint.
  4. Adjust the dimensions if necessary.
  5. Only then cut the complete project.

A few minutes spent making a test piece can prevent wasting an entire sheet of material.

Leave Enough Material

Avoid placing holes, slots, or other features extremely close to the edge of a part. Very thin sections can become fragile, burn away, or break during assembly. If a feature looks delicate in the design, consider increasing the amount of material around it.
